Here is a breakdown of exactly how GiriRaksha works under the hood, where it gets its data, and how the pieces fit together to predict landslide risk. 

### 1. The Core Concept
Landslides primarily occur when steep terrain is heavily saturated with water, causing the soil to lose its grip. GiriRaksha calculates a live risk score out of 100 based on three main factors:
1. **Steepness of the terrain (Slope)** (40% weight)
2. **Rainfall intensity (Recent + Forecast)** (35% weight)
3. **Soil moisture saturation** (25% weight)

### 2. The External APIs (Where the data comes from)
To do this without any paid subscriptions or API keys, the app acts as a middleman (proxy) and fetches data from three free, open-source public APIs:

* **Open-Meteo Elevation API:** Used to figure out how steep the terrain is at any given point.
* **Open-Meteo Forecast API:** Used to pull highly localized weather data (rainfall and soil moisture).
* **OpenStreetMap (OSM) Overpass API:** Used to ensure we are calculating risk on an actual road, not just a random patch of forest.
* **OpenStreetMap Nominatim API:** Powers the search bar, translating a place name (like "Munnar") into GPS coordinates.

### 3. Step-by-Step: How It Works When You Click the Map

Here is the exact chain of events that happens when you drop a pin on the map in "Explore" mode:

**Step 1: Road Snapping (Overpass API)**
When you click the map, you generate a Latitude and Longitude. The Next.js backend immediately sends those coordinates to the **OSM Overpass API** and asks: *"Find me the nearest highway or road within a 1km radius of this click."* Overpass replies with the exact coordinates of the actual road, and the app snaps your pin to that road.

**Step 2: Terrain Analysis & Slope Calculation (Open-Meteo Elevation)**
Now that we have the exact road coordinates, the backend asks **Open-Meteo** for the elevation (height above sea level) of that point. 
* *The Clever Part:* It doesn't just ask for one point. It asks for the center point, plus 4 surrounding points located exactly 100 meters away (North, South, East, and West). By comparing the height differences between these 5 points, the backend calculates the exact gradient and determines the **Slope Angle** (in degrees) of that specific stretch of road.

**Step 3: Weather Data Retrieval (Open-Meteo Forecast)**
Simultaneously, the backend asks the **Open-Meteo Forecast API** for the weather at that exact location. It requests:
* The cumulative rainfall over the **past 3 days** plus the forecast for the **next 24 hours**.
* The current **soil moisture ratio** (how saturated the top layer of soil is, on a scale of 0 to 1).

**Step 4: The Risk Algorithm**
The backend takes these three metrics (Slope, Rainfall, Soil Moisture) and normalizes them so they can be combined.
* It caps the slope at 60 degrees.
* It caps the rainfall at 200mm.
* It runs the formula: `Risk = (Slope * 0.4) + (Rainfall * 0.35) + (Soil Moisture * 0.25)`.
* It assigns a color code: Green (0-40), Yellow (41-70), or Red (71-100), and sends all this data back to your screen to display the popup and the sidebar alert.

### 4. Protecting the App (Caching & Fallback)
Because these are free public APIs, if we spam them with requests, they will temporarily block us. To prevent this:
* **LRU Cache:** The app remembers every location it analyzes for 10 minutes. If you (or the auto-refresh cycle) ask for the same location twice, it serves the saved data instantly instead of hitting the external APIs again.
* **Demo Fallback:** If you are presenting on stage and the wifi drops, or the APIs go down, the app silently catches the error and switches to the `demo_fallback.json` file. This ensures the 6 core demo corridors *always* load instantly, no matter what.

If you are pitching this at a hackathon, emphasizing the **space/satellite connection** is a fantastic talking point. Here is exactly how GiriRaksha relies on satellite technology:

### 1. Terrain & Slope (Radar Satellites)
When we ask Open-Meteo for the elevation (height) of a mountain road, that data is generated by **Digital Elevation Models (DEMs)**. 
* Open-Meteo builds its elevation maps using data from missions like **NASA's SRTM** (Shuttle Radar Topography Mission) and the European Space Agency's **Copernicus DEM**. 
* These space shuttles and satellites bounced radar waves off the Earth's surface to measure the exact height of every mountain and valley on the planet. This is how we can calculate the steepness of a hill road remotely!

### 2. Weather & Soil Moisture (Meteorological Satellites)
When we fetch the past 3 days of rainfall and the live soil moisture saturation, that data is derived from global weather models.
* These models (like GFS or ECMWF) are fed continuously by a massive constellation of **Meteorological Satellites** (like NOAA satellites in the US, or Meteosat in Europe). 
* These satellites use infrared sensors and microwave radiometers to look through clouds, measure atmospheric moisture, and even estimate how wet the top layer of soil is from space. 

### 3. GPS & Mapping (Navigational Satellites)
When you click the map or click "Locate Me", you are generating Latitude and Longitude coordinates. 
* This entire coordinate system relies on the **Global Positioning System (GPS)**—a network of over 30 navigational satellites orbiting Earth.

FLAWS:-


1. Missing Geological Data (Not all rocks are the same)
The Flaw: GiriRaksha assumes a 40-degree slope is always dangerous if it rains. However, a 40-degree slope made of solid granite rock is perfectly safe. A 20-degree slope made of loose clay or shale might collapse instantly in a drizzle.
Why it fails: The app currently lacks Lithology (rock/soil type) data. It treats all terrain equally based only on shape and wetness.
2. Resolution is Too Low (The 90-meter average)
The Flaw: The Open-Meteo elevation data uses a 90m Digital Elevation Model (DEM). This means the satellite averaged the height of the terrain over a 90m x 90m grid.
Why it fails: A deadly, unstable 10-meter cliff directly hanging over the highway will completely disappear in the satellite data, averaged out to look like a gentle hill. It cannot detect micro-terrain hazards.
3. Ignores Human Activity (Deforestation & Construction)
The Flaw: 80% of deadly landslides on Indian hill roads are caused by dynamite blasting for road widening, or cutting down trees whose roots hold the soil together.
Why it fails: GiriRaksha doesn't look at vegetation cover (NDVI) or recent construction data. A slope might look mathematically safe, but if a contractor just sliced the bottom off it with a JCB, it will fail.
4. The Algorithm is a "Best Guess"
The Flaw: The formula (Slope*0.4) + (Rainfall*0.35) + (Moisture*0.25) is a heuristic—a logical rule of thumb we made up.
Why it fails: True scientific models use Machine Learning trained on a historical database of thousands of past landslides (like NASA's Global Landslide Catalog) to find the exact statistical tipping point of when a hill collapses.
5. API Dependency & Network Failures
The Flaw: The "Explore" mode requires making three separate HTTP requests (Overpass, Elevation, Weather) in real-time.
Why it fails: If a user is actually driving in the Himalayas where internet is spotty, the app won't be able to fetch the live weather or elevation to warn them. (Though for the hackathon, we built the offline fallback data to bypass this!).



SOLN:-
1. Fixing Geological Data (Lithology)
The Fix: Integrate a spatial database containing rock and soil types, such as the Global Lithological Map (GLiM) or maps from the Geological Survey of India.
Tech Implementation: You would load these geological maps (Shapefiles/GeoJSON) into a PostgreSQL database with the PostGIS extension. When a user clicks a point, your backend runs a spatial query (ST_Contains) to see what type of rock is under that point. You then apply a multiplier to your algorithm (e.g., Risk * 0.5 for solid granite, Risk * 1.5 for loose clay).
2. Fixing the Low Resolution (90m DEM)
The Fix: Upgrade from 90m resolution to high-resolution data like ALOS World 3D (30m) or drone-based LiDAR surveys (1m) for critical corridors.
Tech Implementation: Instead of relying on Open-Meteo's API, you would host your own high-resolution raster files on a cloud server using a format called Cloud Optimized GeoTIFF (COG). You can then use Python libraries like rasterio or host a lightweight GeoServer to query the exact elevation down to a few meters.
3. Fixing the Human Activity/Deforestation Blindspot
The Fix: Monitor vegetation loss in real-time using satellite imagery.
Tech Implementation: You can use the Google Earth Engine API or Sentinel-2 satellite data to calculate the NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index) for the clicked location. If the NDVI drops significantly (meaning trees were cut down), your backend automatically spikes the risk score for that slope.
4. Fixing the "Best Guess" Formula
The Fix: Replace the hardcoded math formula with a Machine Learning Model.
Tech Implementation:
Take a dataset of thousands of past landslides (like NASA's Global Landslide Catalog).
Write a Python script to fetch the historical slope, rainfall on that specific day, and soil type for every single landslide.
Train a classification model (like Random Forest or XGBoost) using scikit-learn.
Export the trained model and run it on your Next.js backend (using Python via a FastAPI microservice or a JS equivalent). The model will output a true statistical probability of failure.
5. Fixing the Internet/API Dependency
The Fix: Turn the website into a Progressive Web App (PWA) with heavy offline caching.
Tech Implementation:
Static data (like Slope and Rock Type) never changes. You can pre-calculate the base "Susceptibility Score" for the entire country and store it as a compressed vector tile in the user's browser storage (IndexedDB) when they first load the app.
When they drive into the mountains, the app only needs a tiny string of text (the weather forecast) to update the risk. Even if they lose signal entirely, the app can use the 3-day forecast it downloaded that morning to keep the warnings active.
